# Mark Yi Cold Outreach

Write cold outreach that demonstrates agency before asking for attention. The email is the delivery mechanism; the real pitch is useful work made specifically for the recipient.

## Core standard

- Pick one company or person, research them, and prove interest through action. Never “spray and pray.”
- Contact the person who can act: usually the founder, CEO, hiring manager, or head of the relevant department.
- Lead with something already made or done for them: a small prototype, improved version of their tool, qualified lead list, content or growth plan, customer research, audit, analysis, useful introduction, or similarly concrete artifact.
- Make the work genuinely useful. A generic teardown, AI-generated memo, or shallow list of observations is not proof of value.
- Show how the artifact connects to a real goal, problem, product, or opportunity at the recipient's organization.
- Use simple English, specific facts, honest claims, and one clear ask.
- Respect the recipient's time and attention. Keep the email under 200 words; usually aim for 75–140.

If the sender has no credible proof, relevant artifact, or useful insight yet, do not disguise that gap with copy. Briefly propose two or three high-leverage things they could make, then ask whether they want to build one or proceed with a lower-ask relationship email.

## Gather the minimum facts

Before drafting, identify:

- the recipient's name, role, company, and authority to act;
- the exact outcome the sender wants;
- one recent, verifiable detail showing why this person or company was chosen;
- the artifact, action, insight, or resource the sender is giving;
- the sender's strongest relevant credential or proof;
- the smallest useful next step;
- any links, attachment names, availability, or phone number that must be included.

Do not invent missing facts, metrics, relationships, credentials, enthusiasm, or familiarity. If a missing fact would change the pitch, ask for it or leave a conspicuous placeholder such as `[specific result]`.

## Build the email

### Subject line

Make the reader's value or the sender's legitimate relevance visible before the open. Prefer a concrete artifact, useful outcome, credible affiliation, or specific collaboration idea. Keep it truthful and plain; avoid clickbait, fake urgency, vague greetings, and gimmicks.

Examples of subject-line shapes, not copy-and-paste formulas:

- `[artifact] for [company/product]`
- `[specific outcome] idea for [company]`
- `[credible identity] — [specific collaboration]`
- `Improved [thing the recipient built]`

### Body

Default to three visually separate blocks of no more than two sentences each. Do not force the structure when a shorter, more natural email works better.

1. **Who + relevance + offer.** Establish who the sender is with one relevant proof point, then state why they are writing and what they are offering. Ideally the ask and the benefit appear together.
2. **Vision + homework.** Explain what was built, found, or proposed and how the recipient could use it. Include one or two precise details that could only come from real research.
3. **Clear CTA.** Ask for one small next step. Make replying easy and non-entitled; when useful and approved by the sender, explicitly offer a phone/text number and a short time bound.

The email should sound like one capable human writing to another. Use contractions when natural, short sentences, and generous spacing. Prefer numbers, named artifacts, and observable outcomes over claims such as “results,” “impact,” “passion,” or “synergy.”

## Calls to action

Match the ask to the evidence already supplied. Good asks are concrete and easy to answer, for example:

- whether the artifact would be useful to the team;
- a 10–15 minute conversation about one defined idea;
- permission to send or walk through a specific resource;
- the correct person to share the work with.

Do not use vague asks such as “pick your brain,” “explore synergies,” or “connect sometime.” Do not demand a meeting merely because work was provided. In some cases the strongest email asks for nothing and simply shares a useful improvement; use that deliberately when relationship-building is the goal.

## Editing pass

Before returning the draft:

1. Cut it below 200 words and remove any sentence that does not establish relevance, prove value, explain the idea, or enable the next step.
2. Replace abstractions with concrete facts, metrics, examples, or artifact names.
3. Remove fake personalization, empty praise, buzzwords, autobiography, and résumé dumping.
4. Check that the subject offers a truthful reason to open.
5. Check that the research is visible without sounding performative.
6. Check that the benefit to the recipient is clearer than the sender's need.
7. Check that there is exactly one primary ask and that it can be answered quickly.
8. Read it aloud. Rewrite anything that sounds like a template, sales sequence, or AI-generated copy.

## Follow-up

When requested, write one brief follow-up to send after about a week. Add a useful detail, refinement, or new result when possible; do not send a bare “bump.” If there is no response after that, stop and move on to another well-researched target unless the user explicitly requests a different cadence.

Scale by writing distinct emails to 10, 20, or 30 well-chosen companies over time—not by blasting one template to a list.

## Default output

Unless the user requests another format, return:

1. three subject lines, strongest first;
2. the email body;
3. the word count;
4. a single follow-up only if requested;
5. a short “Missing proof” note only when a required fact or value artifact is absent.

Do not include a strategy explanation or send the email unless the user asks.
